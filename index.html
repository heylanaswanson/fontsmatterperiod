Akzidenz-Grotesk — the one that started everything
Before Helvetica. Before Univers. Before the whole Swiss International Style thing became a poster in a design student's dorm, there was Akzidenz-Grotesk. Released in 1896 by the H. Berthold type foundry, this is the quiet original — the one all those modernists were borrowing from when they claimed to be inventing something new.

Neo-Grotesque1957Miedinger
Helvetica — the most famous font in the world, for better and worse
It got a documentary. It's on the New York subway, the American Apparel logo, and approximately every corporate rebrand since 1970. Max Miedinger designed it to mean nothing — to be neutral, invisible, universal. The irony is it's now the most recognizable typeface on earth.

Garamond — five centuries and still the most elegant thing in the room
Claude Garamond cut these letterforms in Paris in the 1530s. They've been in continuous use ever since. If that doesn't tell you something about what timeless design actually looks like, nothing will.

Baskerville
Aa Bb Cc 123
Serif1757Transitional
Baskerville — the typeface Benjamin Franklin tried to debunk
John Baskerville's contemporaries thought his type was harmful to eyesight. Benjamin Franklin used it in a hoax to embarrass a critic. Researchers later found it makes people believe statements more. It is, objectively, one of the most trustworthy things ever drawn.

Futura
Aa Bb Cc 123
Geometric Sans1927Renner
Futura — the font that went to the moon
Literally. The plaque left on the moon by Apollo 11 is set in Futura. Paul Renner designed it as a rejection of ornament — pure geometry, the circle as O, the triangle as A. It looks like the future because it was designed to.

Optima
Aa Bb Cc 123
Humanist Sans1958Zapf
Optima — the typeface that refuses to pick a side
Hermann Zapf designed Optima after sketching letterforms on gravestones in Florence. It has no serifs, but its strokes flare at the terminals like a classical Roman inscription. It is technically a sans-serif that behaves like a serif. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial is set in it.

Papyrus
Aa Bb Cc 123
Display1982Rolston
Papyrus — the font that made a million spa menus illegitimate
Chris Rolston hand-drew Papyrus in 1982 on — wait for it — actual papyrus. It is not a bad typeface. It is a very specific typeface used in every wrong context imaginable: day spas, Avatar, Egyptian themed restaurants in strip malls. The font didn't fail us. We failed the font.

Caslon
Aa Bb Cc 123
Serif1722Old Style
Caslon — the font that founded a nation
The U.S. Declaration of Independence was set in Caslon. So was the Constitution. William Caslon started by cutting letters into gun barrels — and produced a typeface so good that everyone switched immediately. No other typeface has a saying. "When in doubt, use Caslon." That's not a catchphrase. It's a 300-year track record.

Comic Sans
Aa Bb Cc 123
Casual1994Connare
Comic Sans — thirty years of crimes against design
Vincent Connare designed it to look like comic book lettering, for a cartoon dog in a software assistant. It was never meant to leave that context. And yet. It appeared on hospital signs, legal documents, school newsletters, and a Microsoft Bob tutorial. It is the most successfully misused typeface in history, which is an achievement of a kind.

Times
Aa Bb Cc 123
Serif1931Morison
Times New Roman — it wasn't designed to be beautiful. It was designed to be cheap.
Stanley Morison commissioned it for The Times of London in 1931 with one brief: fit more words per column inch. Not elegance. Not prestige. Efficiency. The irony is it became the default typeface of academia, law, and bureaucracy worldwide — a workhorse mistaken for a monument every time someone hits 12pt on a fresh document.

Franklin
Aa Bb Cc 123
Grotesque1902Benton
Franklin Gothic — he designed 221 typefaces. You've never heard his name.
Morris Fuller Benton was the most prolific type designer in American history. He redesigned half the faces in ATF's catalog and created dozens more from scratch. Franklin Gothic — named after Benjamin Franklin for no particular reason — became the heavy workhorse of American newspaper headlines and political campaign posters. Bold, condensed, completely unignorable.

Arial
Aa Bb Cc 123
Neo-Grotesque1982Monotype
Arial — the font that took Helvetica's job. Legally.
Microsoft needed a Helvetica substitute that didn't require a license. Monotype designed Arial to have identical character widths so documents set in one would reflow correctly in the other. Type designers noticed immediately. The general public did not. Arial became the default sans-serif on every Windows machine for fifteen years. The whole time, it was a stand-in.

Didot
Aa Bb Cc 123
Didone1784F. Didot
Didot — everyone says Vogue's logo is Bodoni. It isn't. Here's why the mix-up makes total sense.
Firmin Didot cut these impossibly thin hairlines and extreme thick-thin contrasts in Paris in 1784. Bodoni — designed in Italy around the same time — arrived at similar conclusions independently. They look almost identical to a civilian. Designers will fight you about which is which. Vogue has been using Didot since 1955. This is not a small distinction.

Bodoni
Aa Bb Cc 123
Didone1798G. Bodoni
Bodoni — hard to read. Used by Vogue for over 100 years. Nobody is changing anything.
Giambattista Bodoni designed letters for display, not for body text — the extreme contrast between thick and hairline strokes dazzles the eye at large sizes and turns to mush at small ones. Fashion magazines figured out early that this is a feature, not a bug. Something that looks luxurious at a glance and resists being read too carefully is exactly what the industry needed.

Gill Sans
Aa Bb Cc 123
Humanist Sans1928Eric Gill
Gill Sans — beautiful typeface. Monstrous designer. Still everywhere.
Eric Gill was a sculptor, letterer, and type designer whose work shaped 20th-century British design. He was also, as his own diaries made undeniable, a deeply terrible person. Gill Sans is on the BBC logo. It was the face of British Railways for decades. The type community has never agreed on what to do about the distinction between the work and the man.

Eurostile
Aa Bb Cc 123
Geometric Sans1962Novarese
Eurostile — the typeface that made the future look like a rectangle
Aldo Novarese designed Eurostile in 1962 with squared-off round forms — the corners just barely curve. It looks like something a spacecraft would wear. Which is why it appeared on every science-fiction movie set, television console, and tech product of the 1960s and 70s. The future, as imagined by the Space Age, was Eurostile. Some things age badly. This one aged into an aesthetic.

Gotham
Aa Bb Cc 123
Geometric Sans2000Frere-Jones
Gotham — the font that won a presidential election
Tobias Frere-Jones based Gotham on the vernacular lettering of mid-century New York — the painted signs, the building facades, the no-nonsense block lettering of a city that doesn't have time for flourishes. The Obama 2008 campaign used it for "HOPE." After that, every political campaign, startup, and municipality in America wanted to look trustworthy and modern. Gotham was the answer to the question for fifteen years.

Trajan
Aa Bb Cc 123
Display Serif1989Twombly
Trajan — carved in stone. Now on every movie poster.
Carol Twombly drew Trajan from the inscriptions on Trajan's Column in Rome — letterforms carved around 113 AD, considered by many the peak of Roman monumental lettering. It has no lowercase. It was designed for grandeur. Hollywood noticed. From Titanic to Gladiator to pretty much any film that needs to feel epic and important, the poster is probably in Trajan.

Copper
Aa Bb Cc 123
Display1901Goudy
Copperplate Gothic — small caps only. No excuses.
Frederic Goudy designed Copperplate Gothic in 1901 with no lowercase — every character is a capital, with tiny incised serifs that look like they were cut into a metal plate. It was built for engraved stationery, business cards, and law firm letterheads. It communicates authority, permanence, and a certain inability to relax. It is still on more business cards than almost anything else ever designed.

Poppins
Aa Bb Cc 123
Geometric Sans2014ITF
Poppins — on a trillion screens. Never on the list.
Six people drew it in-house at Indian Type Foundry between 2013 and 2016. Google Fonts analytics puts it eighth all-time. Typewolf tracks three thousand curated design sites and it isn't in the top fifteen. A font nobody paid for doesn't read as a decision — it reads as what was already there. The design is fine. The credit problem is the story.
