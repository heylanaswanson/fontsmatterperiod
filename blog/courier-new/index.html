Howard Kettler drew Courier for IBM's typewriters in 1955. IBM never locked it down, so it spread to every competitor, every machine, and eventually every operating system on earth. It is the house font of this entire site, and the reason a screenwriter can hand you 110 pages and tell you the film runs an hour and fifty.

It was built for a machine, not for a page

A typewriter carriage advances the same distance after every key, so every character has to occupy the same width. That is the entire constraint, and it is a brutal one. An i gets the same room as an m. Kettler's answer was to let the narrow letters sit in generous space and give the wide ones flat, spreading serifs to fill their slot. Nothing in Courier is decorative. Every oddity in it — the gappy lowercase, the slab serifs on the i, the wide-open counters — is a solution to a mechanical problem.

IBM never locked it down

This is the part that matters. IBM had the design and did not secure exclusive rights to it, and Courier escaped into the industry. Competing typewriter manufacturers used it. Then it became a standard on daisy-wheel printers, then a core font on the Macintosh and Windows, then the default for plain text and code almost everywhere. One of the most widely distributed typefaces of the twentieth century generated effectively nothing for the company that commissioned it. Whether that was generosity or an oversight depends on who is telling the story.

One page, one minute

Screenplays are set in 12-point Courier, and the reason is arithmetic rather than taste. Because every character is the same width and the margins are fixed by convention, a correctly formatted page holds a near-constant amount of dialogue and action — which converts to roughly one minute of screen time. That is why a reader can weigh a script in their hand and know the runtime, and why every studio still rejects submissions in anything else. It is the only typeface in wide use whose job is to measure time.

Courier is not Courier New

The Courier on your computer is probably not Kettler's. Monotype redrew it as Courier New, thinning the strokes considerably, and that is the cut bundled with Windows and the one most people have actually read. Set the two side by side and the original looks like it was struck onto the page while the New looks like a photocopy of it. If you want Kettler's weight, you want Courier Std or Nimbus Mono. This is the single most common substitution nobody notices.

Why this site wears it, and where it doesn't

A typeface built entirely from constraints, with zero ornament, is the clearest possible argument that typography is not decoration. Courier has no mood to sell you. So it is the voice of this site — the logo, the labels, the tags, the eyebrows, every part that tells you where you are. It is not what you are reading right now, because monospace is a poor surface for a thousand words and pretending otherwise would be exactly the unexamined default this whole entry is about. Using a typeface for what it is actually good at is not a betrayal of it. That is the entire skill.

Specimen above is set in Courier New (system) — the real thing, not a stand-in. Where we can't license a typeface for web use, we say so.
