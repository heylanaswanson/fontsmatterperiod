Not a neutral overview. Every typeface covered here gets an opinion, a history, and an argument. We're not here to list — we're here to take a position.

No. 001
Garamond
Aa Bb Cc · 1530
SerifOld Style1530
500 years old. Still everywhere.
Claude Garamond cut these letters for the French king. They became the most copied typeface in history. 18+ versions exist — none are exact. The most common one (ITC Garamond) is considered a distortion. Adobe Garamond Pro and EB Garamond are the ones that got it right.

No. 002
Comic Sans
Aa Bb Cc · 1994
Casual1994Controversial
The most hated font in the world. Let's talk about why that's wrong.
Vincent Connare designed it in a weekend — because he was annoyed that a cartoon dog in Microsoft Bob was speaking in Times New Roman. It was never meant to leave that context. NASA used it for the Curiosity Mars rover announcement anyway. Make of that what you will.

No. 003
Baskerville
Aa Bb Cc · 1757
SerifTransitional1757
The font that makes you believe things.
John Baskerville invented his own ink, redesigned the printing press, and redesigned the paper — all to serve this typeface. In 2012, filmmaker Errol Morris ran an experiment in the New York Times: the same statement rated more true when set in Baskerville than any other font tested. He died thinking he'd failed.

No. 004
Papyrus
Aa Bb Cc · 1982
Display1982Font on trial
Guilty. With a footnote.
Chris Costello drew it by hand over six months — on actual papyrus. Genuinely impressive craft. Then Microsoft bundled it into Office in 1996. Then James Cameron used it for Avatar in 2009. Ryan Gosling never recovered. Neither did we. The font is not the problem. The choices were.

No. 005
FUTURA
Aa Bb Cc · 1927
Geometric Sans1927Bauhaus
The font that went to the moon.
Literally. The plaque left on the lunar surface by Apollo 11 is set in Futura. Paul Renner designed it in 1927 as pure geometry — circles, triangles, lines. No calligraphic history, no human hand, just math. NASA, IKEA, Volkswagen, Louis Vuitton. Still running the world.

No. 006
Caslon
Aa Bb Cc · 1722
SerifOld Style1722
The font that founded a nation.
The U.S. Declaration of Independence was set in Caslon. So was the Constitution. William Caslon started by cutting letters into gun barrels — and produced a typeface so good that everyone switched to it immediately. No other typeface has a saying. "When in doubt, use Caslon." That's a 300-year track record of working in almost every context.

No. 007
Akzidenz
Aa Bb Cc · 1896
Sans-serifGrotesque1896
Before Helvetica. Before all of it.
Released in 1896 by the H. Berthold type foundry in Berlin, Akzidenz-Grotesk is the quiet original — the one all those Swiss modernists were borrowing from when they claimed to be inventing something new. Helvetica didn't appear until 1957. That's 61 years of Akzidenz doing the work first.

No. 008
Helvetica
Aa Bb Cc · 1957
Neo-Grotesque1957Miedinger
The most famous font in the world. For better and for worse.
It got a documentary. It's on the New York subway, the American Apparel logo, and approximately every corporate rebrand since 1970. Max Miedinger designed it to mean nothing — to be neutral, invisible, universal. The irony is it's now the most recognizable typeface on earth. Invisibility failed spectacularly.

No. 009
Optima
Aa Bb Cc · 1958
Humanist Sans1958Zapf
The typeface that refuses to pick a side.
Hermann Zapf sketched the letterforms on gravestones in Florence in 1950. Optima has no serifs — but its strokes flare at the terminals like a classical Roman inscription. It is technically a sans-serif that behaves like a serif. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial is set in it. So is the Estée Lauder logo. Make of that what you will.

No. 010
Times
Aa Bb Cc · 1931
Serif1931Morison
It was never meant to be beautiful. It was meant to be cheap.
The Times of London commissioned it in 1931 with one brief: fit more words per column inch. Not elegance. Not prestige. Economy. Stanley Morison directed it, Victor Lardent drew it, and it first set the paper the following year. It then became the default of academia, law, and bureaucracy worldwide — a cost-cutting measure mistaken for a standard.

No. 011
Franklin
Aa Bb Cc · 1902
Grotesque1902Benton
He designed 221 typefaces. You've never heard his name.
Morris Fuller Benton was the most prolific type designer in American history — head of design at American Type Founders for over three decades. Franklin Gothic, named for Benjamin Franklin, became the voice of the American newspaper headline and the political poster. Heavy, condensed, impossible to ignore. Benton got no credit on any of it.

No. 012
Arial
Aa Bb Cc · 1982
Neo-Grotesque1982Monotype
The font that took Helvetica's job. Legally.
Monotype needed a Helvetica that nobody had to license. Robin Nicholas and Patricia Saunders built Arial with identical character widths, so text set in one reflows correctly in the other — metrically a clone, visually a near-miss. Microsoft shipped it with Windows 3.1 in 1992. Designers noticed immediately. Everyone else never did.

No. 013
Didot
Aa Bb Cc · 1784
Didone1784F. Didot
Everyone says Vogue's logo is Bodoni. It isn't. The mix-up makes total sense.
Firmin Didot cut these hairline serifs and extreme stroke contrasts in Paris. Giambattista Bodoni reached nearly identical conclusions in Italy at nearly the same time, working independently. The two faces are the reason an entire category — Didone — is named after both of them. Vogue has used a Didot since the 1950s. Designers will still correct you about it.

No. 014
Bodoni
Aa Bb Cc · 1798
Didone1798G. Bodoni
Hard to read. Used by fashion for a century anyway. Nobody is changing it.
Giambattista Bodoni drew letters for display, not for paragraphs. The violent jump between thick stems and hairline serifs dazzles at poster size and dissolves into glare at body size. Fashion publishing worked out early that this is the point: something that looks expensive at a glance and resists being read too closely is exactly the brief.

No. 015
Gill Sans
Aa Bb Cc · 1928
Humanist Sans1928Eric Gill
Beautiful typeface. Monstrous designer. Still everywhere.
Eric Gill studied under Edward Johnston, whose Underground lettering shaped London, then drew Gill Sans for Monotype in 1928. It became British Railways, Penguin Books, and the BBC. A 1989 biography drawing on his own diaries established that he was also a serial abuser. The type community has never resolved what to do with that, and the typeface is still on your screen.

No. 016
Eurostile
Aa Bb Cc · 1962
Geometric Sans1962Novarese
The typeface that made the future a rectangle.
Aldo Novarese took Microgramma — a caps-only face he'd built a decade earlier — and gave it a lowercase. The result squares off every curve just enough to read as engineered rather than drawn. Every spacecraft console, sci-fi title card, and hi-fi faceplate of the next twenty years used it. The Space Age had a typeface, and this was it.

No. 017
Gotham
Aa Bb Cc · 2000
Geometric Sans2000Frere-Jones
The font that won a presidential election.
GQ commissioned Tobias Frere-Jones to find a typeface in New York itself — the painted signs, the building facades, the lettering of the Port Authority Bus Terminal. No designer had drawn it; the city had. Then the Obama campaign set HOPE in it in 2008, and every candidate, startup, and city government in America wanted to sound exactly that trustworthy.

No. 018
Trajan
Aa Bb Cc · 1989
Display Serif1989Twombly
Carved in stone in 113 AD. Now on every movie poster.
Carol Twombly drew Trajan from the inscription at the base of Trajan's Column in Rome — letterforms cut around 113 AD and still considered the high point of Roman monumental capitals. There is no lowercase, because there was no lowercase. Hollywood found it and never let go. If a poster needs to feel important, this is the shortcut.

No. 019
Copper
Aa Bb Cc · 1901
Display1901Goudy
Capitals only. No excuses.
Frederic Goudy gave Copperplate Gothic serifs so small they read less as serifs than as the mark a graver leaves in metal — which is the whole idea, since it was built for engraved stationery. There is no true lowercase. It has communicated authority, permanence, and a certain inability to relax from law firm letterheads for over a century.

No. 020
Courier
Aa Bb Cc · 1955
Monospace1955Kettlerhouse font
Every screenplay you have ever loved is set in this. One page, one minute.
Howard Kettler drew Courier for IBM's typewriters in 1955. IBM never locked it down, so it spread to every competitor, every machine, and eventually every operating system on earth. It is the house font of this entire site, and the reason a screenwriter can hand you 110 pages and tell you the film runs an hour and fifty.

On a trillion screens. Never on the list.
